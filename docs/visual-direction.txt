FRANKENTOWER — Visual Direction

The current prototype should be redesigned away from the appearance of a management application.

FRANKENTOWER is a pixel-art monster-building dungeon autobattler built around watching small arena battles. It should look and feel like a game first. Stats, equipment and economy support the game world rather than becoming the dominant visual language.

Primary Visual Principle

The creature and the arena battle are the centre of the experience.

Each ordinary floor is essentially a small combat arena:

* one roughly circular dungeon room
* stairs/door providing context for entering and leaving
* perhaps a few pieces of furniture, rubble, pillars, torches or other decoration
* the player’s creature
* the enemies generated for that encounter

We are not trying to make elaborate dungeon exploration.

The room exists primarily to give the creatures a physical space in which to fight.

During combat, the majority of the screen should therefore be the arena.

The player should primarily be watching:

* their creature move around the room
* enemies move and reposition
* creatures approach and avoid each other
* melee attackers close distance
* creatures dodge or move away from threats
* attacks visibly connect
* heavy attacks wind up
* enemies die
* the player’s increasingly ridiculous anatomy participate in the fight

This should feel like watching a little arena battle, not watching two stat blocks exchange damage.

Even though combat is automatic, the creatures should appear to be actively fighting.

They should move around each other, acquire targets, approach, retreat where appropriate, attack, react to impacts and reposition.

A creature wielding a Dagger should dart around attacking rapidly.

A creature carrying an enormous Maul should visibly pursue a target, wind up and deliver a huge slow blow.

Eventually, a bizarre high-level build might be racing around the room on eight legs while swinging several weapons and triggering attacks at absurd speed.

Watching that happen is a major part of the reward.

The arena should consume roughly 60–70% of the player’s visual attention during a fight.

The interface should frame the battle rather than compete with it.

Avoid a layout dominated by:

* dashboards
* grids of rectangular cards
* large stat panels
* application-style navigation
* tables
* bordered information boxes
* excessive buttons
* large quantities of persistent text

If the game could plausibly be mistaken for a project-management tool when the sprites are removed, the UI has become too dominant.

Arena Layout

For the initial game, ordinary floors can reuse essentially the same circular room.

That is intentional.

FRANKENTOWER is not primarily a dungeon-layout game. The interesting variation comes from:

* the player’s current monster
* enemy composition
* movement
* attack cadence
* equipment interactions
* anatomy
* combat effects

The arena can contain a small amount of decorative variation:

* crates
* tables
* chairs
* bones
* statues
* bookcases
* rugs
* laboratory equipment
* increasingly elaborate furnishings higher in the tower

Most of this can initially be non-interactive.

Do not spend significant development effort creating unique floor layouts before the core arena combat feels good to watch.

The simple room should instead become a stage on which increasingly absurd fights occur.

Spatial Combat

Combat should be genuinely spatial even though the player does not directly control it.

Creatures have positions and move through the arena.

Melee attacks require creatures to get close enough to attack.

Movement Speed therefore has an observable physical meaning.

A one-legged creature should visibly struggle to move compared with a normal human.

A four-legged creature should be noticeably mobile.

An eight-legged monstrosity should be capable of scuttling around the arena at alarming speed.

This makes anatomy readable through behaviour rather than merely through statistics.

Later Ranged and Magical attacks can make positioning more strategically important:

* ranged creatures can kite slower opponents
* melee creatures need to close distance
* fast creatures can pursue
* obstacles can eventually affect movement or line of sight if worthwhile

Those systems are not necessary for the first playable version.

For now, the important requirement is simply:

Creatures should visibly move around and fight each other rather than standing in assigned positions exchanging attacks.

Movement does not need sophisticated tactical AI.

Simple behaviours are sufficient if they produce lively, readable battles.

Visual Style

Use a deliberately chunky dark fantasy pixel-art presentation.

Target the feel of an old dungeon game rather than a modern web application.

The dungeon should use:

* dark stone
* pools of warm light
* heavy shadows
* arches, stairs, doors and dungeon props
* restrained colour outside characters/effects
* simple but atmospheric animation

The game can be grotesque, but it should not be visually miserable. There is humour and energy underneath the horror.

Avoid sleek sci-fi UI, glass panels, gradients, productivity-app styling and modern SaaS aesthetics.

Combat Screen

Combat should be visually dominated by the dungeon room.

The player’s creature should occupy a prominent position and be large enough that its changing silhouette matters.

Enemies physically occupy the arena rather than appearing as rows, cards or opposing stat panels.

Minimal persistent information can sit around the edges:

* HP
* Mana where relevant
* Circulation warning where relevant
* current floor
* gold where relevant
* perhaps small equipment activation indicators

Do not permanently display every statistic simply because the simulation contains it.

Combat information should appear in the world where practical:

* damage numbers over targets
* healing numbers
* Shield appearing around the creature
* Armour communicated through impacts/effects
* Poison/Bleed status icons near the affected creature
* Crits visually distinct from normal hits
* item activations briefly identifiable

The player needs to understand why the machine is working, but that information should feel like combat feedback rather than an analytics dashboard.

Creature Presentation

The monster is the game’s visual star.

The player begins as an ordinary human.

As anatomy changes, the silhouette should become progressively less human.

Do not require perfect modular rendering of every anatomical configuration for the first version.

Representative body forms are acceptable:

* human
* extra-armed
* multi-headed
* quadrupedal
* many-legged
* headless
* blob
* extreme monstrosity

Equipment should be visible where practical, particularly iconic equipment such as:

* enormous weapons
* shields
* helmets
* multiple shoes

The player should occasionally look at the creature and think:

“What the fuck have I done?”

Equipment Screen

Avoid presenting equipment primarily as an RPG spreadsheet.

The creature should remain central.

Equipment slots should be spatially associated with anatomy where practical:

* Hand equipment near arms
* Head equipment near heads
* Foot equipment near legs/feet
* Chest equipment near torso

An extra arm should visually feel like gaining somewhere to put another weapon, rather than merely causing “Hand Slot +1” to appear in a list.

Detailed stats can appear contextually when selecting or inspecting an item.

Shop

The shop should feel like a place where objects are being offered, not an ecommerce page.

Items can be presented visually on:

* shelves
* a counter
* hooks
* stands

Price and essential effect text should be immediately readable.

Full details can appear on selection/inspection.

The creature should preferably remain visible so buying equipment feels like modifying the thing that will shortly fight.

Reroll, shop upgrade and gold are important, but should not visually overwhelm the items.

Surgery / Scientist Screen

This can be more interface-heavy because the medical interface is part of the fiction.

Tone: clinical, calm, helpful.

The scientist is not a cackling mad scientist. He behaves like a competent physician.

Opening line:

“You’ll never make it to the top with your body like that. Here, let me help…”

Procedures should look like medical options rather than magical power selections.

Possible presentation:

* creature/body diagram
* anatomical highlighting
* procedure tray
* clinical labels
* simple before/after consequences

Language should be reassuringly medical even when the procedure is grotesque.

Example:

Additional Upper Limb

Graft a compatible donor arm.

+100 Max HP
+1 Hand Slot
+Circulatory Load

The humour comes from treating this as normal medicine.

Tone

The game takes its absurdity seriously.

Do not fill the interface with jokes explaining that the creature is ridiculous.

The world is practical, clinical and matter-of-fact.

A monster with six arms is not remarkable because six arms are funny.

Six arms mean:

* six Hand slots
* increased HP
* increased Circulatory Load
* potentially inadequate cardiovascular support

The game discusses those consequences professionally.

Achievements and occasional item names can be much more playful.

Animation and Feel

Combat should have physical impact despite simple pixel art.

Prioritise:

* readable anticipation before heavy attacks
* movement before attacks rather than stationary attack trading
* small recoil on hits
* brief hit-stop for enormous impacts
* screen shake used sparingly for genuinely large attacks
* clear attack arcs/projectiles
* satisfying death animations
* obvious Shield break
* clear Crit feedback
* visible status application

A Heavy Maul activating should feel completely different from a Dagger activation.

If a build eventually gets the Heavy Maul activating several times per second, the resulting visual absurdity is desirable.

The ideal combat experience is that the player can watch their build happen.

Numbers tell the player that their build works.

The arena should show them what that build actually looks like when released into a room full of monsters.

Information Hierarchy

At any moment the screen should answer, in roughly this order:

1. What is happening to my monster?
2. What is it fighting?
3. Is my build working?
4. What can I change next?
5. What are the exact numbers?

The current prototype should prioritise the first three.

Exact numerical detail should remain available through inspection rather than occupying the entire screen continuously.

First Visual-Pass Goal

Do not attempt final art.

The immediate goal is to make the prototype read unmistakably as:

a dark pixel-art dungeon game about watching your increasingly horrible monster fight little arena battles

rather than:

a web application for managing a monster build.

Use placeholders freely where necessary.

Composition, hierarchy, movement, atmosphere and interaction feel matter more at this stage than polished assets.
